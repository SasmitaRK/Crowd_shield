Open-source crowd-safety AI · built and tested for Indian crowds

See a crowd crush coming, before it happens.

Crowd Shield counts every head in CCTV, drone and phone images, turns the count into people per square metre on a satellite map, watches camera feeds for zones filling up or surging, and plans where to move people. These are the warning signs crowd-safety research says appear minutes before a crush.

Why this matters

Crushes don't come from nowhere

In a crush, people die standing up, squeezed until they can't breathe. Density and pressure build for minutes before that happens. Crowd Shield measures both, using cameras that are already there.

Maha Kumbh, Prayagraj · 29 January 2025
30+ killed

On the Mauni Amavasya bathing day, barricades gave way near the Sangam Nose and the crowd surged (Uttar Pradesh Police).

Chithirai festival, Madurai · 16 April 2022
2 killed

More than five lakh people gathered for Lord Kallazhagar's entry into the Vaigai river, and a crush broke out on the riverbank (police reports).

The number that matters is people per square metre

A head count alone can't tell you if a crowd is dangerous. You also need the ground area.

How it works

From a camera frame to an evacuation plan

1

Count every head

A point model (PET) puts a dot on each head, even where bodies overlap. A density model and YOLO are the fallbacks.

2

Measure the ground

Zones drawn on satellite imagery give the real area in m², and a ground calibration does the same for a camera view.

3

Read the danger

Density sets the risk level. On video, optical flow tracks speed and crowd pressure, and an alarm fires on a sudden surge.

4

Act

An optimiser plans where to move people, only along routes they can walk and without overfilling any zone, and alerts each zone's team by Telegram or email.

India test set

105 photos of melas, festivals, stations and temples

All freely licensed from Wikimedia Commons, with credits. On the first 10 hand-checked photos the first model scored 66.7%, because it took railway gravel for heads. The second, also trained on 1,200 dense crowd photos and on empty Indian tracks, scores 91% (86% on the free online server).

What I learned

Where it fails, and what I did about it

A safety tool that hides its failures is dangerous. These are published in full.

Gravel looks like heads

From above, railway ballast reads as a sea of heads: 1,262 counted where there were 415. Retraining fixed that photo but hurt the others, so I kept the better model and documented the failure.

Phones move

All 15 freely licensed Indian crowd clips I found were filmed handheld, so speed readings measured the camera, not the crowd. The app now detects a moving camera and hides motion results instead of guessing.

No GPU, still useful

On a plain 4-core CPU, PET takes about 16 s per photo. The free server has 3.2 GB, so it counts big photos in overlapping pieces: same accuracy, about 30 seconds each. A live feed is recounted every 30 s or more; video-rate analysis would need a GPU.

Field test · Indian festival video

Counting works on handheld Indian clips (Holi in Howrah, Bhagoriya, Gangasagar). Motion analysis needs a fixed camera, such as CCTV.
